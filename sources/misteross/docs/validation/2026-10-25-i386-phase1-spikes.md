# i386 Phase 1 first slice: length decode, front end and timing spikes

Dated record, 2026-10-25. Host simulation and routed diagnostics on Powerboat.
No package, RBF or kit. Design: [i386 CPU design](../i386-cpu-design.md).
RTL: [`cores/fes-common/rtl/i386`](../../cores/fes-common/rtl/i386/README.md).
Run data: [`2026-10-25-i386-phase1-spikes.jsonl`](2026-10-25-i386-phase1-spikes.jsonl),
one line per route with its argv, tools, GPU, fmax, utilization and
critical-path summary. The routes ran at `e5dcf6cae2`'s RTL; the harness then
only changed how it detects an M10K-launched path, and the summaries were
recomputed from each route's `timing.json` with that version.

**Correction, later on 2026-10-25.** The spike netlists below came from
Yosys 3a5accbd mis-reading the length decoder: a struct-returning helper
called from another function read as empty, so the decoders lacked the ModRM
and immediate bytes of the opcodes built with it (80-83, 69, 6B, C0, C1, C6,
C7, F6, F7, 0F 20-26, 0F A4, 0F AC, 0F BA). Simulating Yosys's netlist against
Bochs gives 10.6 million mismatches per default size; Verilator's reading of
the RTL, which the checks below used, was right. `fes_i386_oplen.sv` now sets
the fields directly and the netlist matches Bochs on every case. The first-slice
front-end figures below are for that smaller decoder. The corrected decoder had not been routed at the time, so its timing was
unmeasured. The later Phase 1 cuts record remeasurement of the revised stages.
The Yosys netlist comparison here was a one-off; the cut record reports checks
rerun through the committed runner's --yosys mode.

## Length decode against the oracle

Verilator, `scripts/test_fes_i386_frontend.py`, oracle Bochs `REL_3_1_FINAL`
at CPU level 3 through the Phase 0 adapter's new `decode()`. A case is
compared where Bochs defines the encoding; for LOCK, the encoding without
LOCK. Bochs raises #UD for the rest, so their length is not architectural.

| Check | Space | Cases | Compared | Oracle #UD | Mismatches |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 16 per-byte decoders | Exhaustive: 12 prefix choices (none or one of 11) × 500 opcodes (244 one-byte, 256 after 0Fh) × 256 ModRM × 256 SIB × 2 default sizes | 786,432,000 | 478,175,232 | 308,256,768 | 0 |
| Prefix runs, MOV TR6/TR7 | Exhaustive: every prefix pair, and 0F 24/26 with every ModRM, in each of the 16 decoders | 10,128 | 10,128 | — | 0 |
| Whole front end | Sampled: 14 streams (below), seed 1 | 4,400,000 | 4,297,603 | 72,655 | 0 |
| AG address unit | Sampled: random and 16-bit wrap-edge operands, both address sizes, against the architectural address | 4,000,000 | 4,000,000 | — | 0 |

The sampled streams, each in 16- and 32-bit code: random bytes (400,000
instructions), random bytes with D stalling 30% of cycles (400,000), memory
30% prefixes (400,000), runs of 8-20 prefixes (100,000), and generated
defined instructions with 0-4 random prefixes (400,000), none (400,000) and
none but 8 bytes or longer (100,000; 5 bytes or longer in 16-bit code, whose
longest prefix-free instruction is 6). Every instruction Q issues is checked at Q's own linear
address for length, prefixes, opcode-aligned bytes and the 15-byte limit.
The remaining 29,742 instructions were over 15 bytes, and Q flagged every
one `too_long`. 14,062 had a 0Fh opcode; 1,055,835 had prefixes, 23,963 of
them 15 or more. The AG check had 1,556,955 vectors with a 16-bit wrap.

Two oracle facts surfaced:

- Bochs at CPU level 3 defines 0F 0D (NOP Ev) and 0F B9 (UD1) with a ModRM.
  The i386 raises #UD for both; the decoders give them the oracle's length.
- Bochs below CPU level 6 dereferences null decoding 0F 38 and 0F 3A. The
  adapter's `decode()` reports both as #UD without decoding; `step()` still
  crashes on them. No corpus case reaches them.

## Throughput

`frontend_tb.cpp`, D always ready, every issue cycle checked exactly:

| Stream | Instructions | Cycles from first to last issue | Timing failures |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Prefix-free, 16-bit | 400,000 | 400,000 | 0 |
| Prefix-free, 32-bit | 400,000 | 400,000 | 0 |
| Prefix-free, 8+ bytes (32-bit) / 5+ bytes (16-bit) | 100,000 each | 100,000 each | 0 |
| 0-4 prefixes, 16-bit | 400,000 | 881,763 | 0 |
| 0-4 prefixes, 32-bit | 400,000 | 879,280 | 0 |

Prefix-free code issues one instruction per cycle, including streams of
nothing but 8- to 11-byte instructions. With prefixes, each instruction
issued max(1, k) cycles after the one before it, k being its prefix count:
the last prefix is free and each earlier one costs a cycle. A stream is laid
out in batches of up to 60 KB, each reached by a redirect; cycles count from
each batch's first issue to its last, and a batch's first instruction is not
timed.

## Timing spikes

`scripts/benchmark_fes_i386.py`: each block in a stimulus and signature
envelope (one clock, KEY0, LED0), `synth_intel_alm -family cyclonev
-nolutram` (M10K and DSP allowed), nextpnr-mistral `--router gpu` through
`gpu-run` with `NEXTPNR_REQUIRE_GPU=1`, `--freq 100 --timing-allow-fail`,
seeds 1-4, HIP on Powerboat (RX 7900 XTX and AI PRO R9700, uncontended).
Two authenticated locks, same netlists:

- `toolchain.lock`, the repository lock: Yosys `3a5accbd`, Mistral
  `8fcc4cb4`, nextpnr `9128800f`. This nextpnr predates the physical
  LUT-input timing model (DeanoC/nextpnr#172, #179, #267) and the TimeQuest
  M10K clock-to-out (#232).
- `cores/fes-spectrum/toolchain.lock`, the newest authenticated lock: the
  same Yosys and Mistral, nextpnr `bd7a1c0d`, which has that model. The
  RISC-V CPU's lock (nextpnr `8f453ebc`) also has #267.

Routed fmax (MHz), seeds 1, 2, 3, 4; the target is 100 MHz:

| Spike | nextpnr `9128800f` (`toolchain.lock`) | nextpnr `bd7a1c0d` (corrected LUT and M10K timing) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Front end | 86.13, 86.98, 84.44, 88.05 — missed | 69.97, 74.35, 72.47, 69.74 — missed |
| AG | 97.49, 87.90, 91.11, 95.45 — missed | 78.17, 80.65, 80.71, 77.36 — missed |
| M2 | 146.33, 135.52, 144.65, 150.65 — met | 102.23, 102.52, 109.69, 103.48 — met |

Every route logged a live HIP backend, and none fell back to the CPU. Each
route had a GPU to itself: two harness runs shared the box, one GPU each.
Routes took 7-100 s.

Most of the difference is the timing model, not the routes: an M10K's
clock-to-out is 1.00 ns in `9128800f` and 2.70 ns in `bd7a1c0d`, and the LUT
delays on these critical paths rise from 0.10-0.51 ns to 0.27-0.78 ns. Take
the `bd7a1c0d` column as the design figure.

| Spike | COMB | FF | M10K | Notes |

The front end alone is about 3,700 COMB cells before L1I, BTB and return
stack. Its design budget for all of those is 3,000 ALMs (6,000 COMB).

### Critical paths and why

**Front end.** Under `9128800f` the worst path is Q's loop: the prefix
accumulator (`acc`) feeds every buffer position's operand- and address-size
select, then the length and in-buffer checks, the 32:1 select by `start`, and
the issue decision, back into `acc` and `acc_linear`. That is 11.5-11.8 ns in
six or seven LUTs, three quarters of it routing. Under `bd7a1c0d` the worst
path is F2: the memory model's M10K data-out (2.70 ns clock-to-out), the
per-byte length decoders (seven LUT levels), the per-byte length registers.
That is 13.4-14.3 ns. Q's loop is not the worst path under this model, so its
own figure there is unmeasured. The F2 path breaks no
stated rule, since there is no carry chain after the M10K, but seven LUT levels
after an M10K output do not fit 10 ns. A real F2 puts the L1I tag compare and
way select ahead of the decoders as well. The design reserved spare M10K bits
for predecode marks "in case routed timing shows the length loop needs them".
This is that measurement.

**AG.** Under both models: a forwarding select register, the 5:1 forwarding
mux, the scale shift and the two 3:2 rows (four or five LUT levels), one
32-bit carry chain (linear or offset), then the 16-bit-wrap compare and
high-half select (two LUT levels), into the M1 register. The M1 early result
feeds back through the forwarding mux, as back-to-back dependent addresses
do. That is 10.3-11.4 ns under `9128800f` and 12.4-12.9 ns under `bd7a1c0d`.
It keeps the rule of one carry chain per stage; the logic around the chain is
the problem.

**M2.** The tag M10K's data-out, a 20-bit compare with the registered page,
the four-way hit, the AND-OR way select, the M2/EX register. That is
6.6-7.4 ns under `9128800f` and 9.1-9.8 ns under `bd7a1c0d`, where the M10K
clock-to-out alone is 2.70 ns. It meets 100 MHz under both, with 2-10% margin
under the corrected model.

### Not changed here

No architecture changed in this slice. Options the measurements point at,
for a later decision:

- Front end, F2: decode from the buffer registers in a stage of its own
  (one more cycle of fetch latency), or store per-byte predecode in the
  M10K spare bits the design reserves for it. A real F2 also puts the L1I tag
  compare and way select ahead of the decoders.
- Front end, Q: move the per-position work out of the loop by storing, at
  fill time, the next start for each combination of accumulated 66h/67h with
  CS.D baked in (a CS.D change already flushes), leaving the loop a 32:1
  select and a 4:1 select.
- AG: the forwarding mux, scale and two 3:2 rows before the carry chain are
  four or five LUT levels, and the wrap select two after it. Forwarding into D's
  output register, or the scale shift in D, removes levels before the chain.
- M2 has margin under both models.

Implementation fixes made while measuring, none of which changes the
architecture: small sums in the decoders and Q are built from gates so they
map to LUTs (Yosys maps every `+` to an ALM carry chain, about 1 ns to enter);
Q's output register loads whenever D can take it, so the issue decision drives
only `q_valid`; and the AG's late operand, the forwarded and scaled index,
enters the last 3:2 row. The first two took the front end from 69.03 to
86.13 MHz on seed 1 under `9128800f`; the third took the AG from 82.81 to
97.49 MHz on seed 1. Both earlier figures are from trial routes before the
runs above.
